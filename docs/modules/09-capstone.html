The rubric

Figma's 2026 report put the problem precisely: designers often cannot prove whether AI output is good or bad. A rubric is how you prove it. Score every page and every piece of copy before it goes anywhere.

Disclosure and originality

You will be asked, by clients and by your own conscience, how much of this was "done by AI". Have a position before you are asked. The legal floor, as it stands in late 2026: the EU AI Act's transparency rules (Article 50) have applied since August 2026. AI-generated text published to inform the public must be disclosed unless it has been through human review with someone holding editorial responsibility — which, if you run this playbook, it always has. Evidently creative work can be disclosed in a way that does not spoil it. The US Copyright Office's 2025 position is that AI assistance does not bar copyright in a human-authored work, but elements the machine decided are not protected. Writers' organisations ask for disclosure of substantial AI generation and treat imitating a named living creator's style as unethical.

A studio policy that meets all of that in five lines:

  1. A human holds editorial control over everything we publish.
  2. We tell clients when AI generated a substantial part of a deliverable.
  3. We never imitate a named living creator's voice or style.
  4. We keep the prompts and context files for every client deliverable.
  5. We check the licence of every image tool before commercial use.

Exercise: the whole thing

  1. Run the project through all eight stages. Do not skip the gates even when the output looks fine; the gates are the method.
  2. Score the result against the rubric. Record scores in docs/runbooks/review-log.md.
  3. Have Claude draft the runbook, then correct it. Add your disclosure policy to the repository README.
  4. Go back to your Module 0 process audit. Rewrite it as it is now. The difference is what you learned.
